# 02. Trust — you can check it, not just believe it

**We hold:**

1. Trust is something you can check, not something we ask you to feel.
2. Every claim we make comes with the proof behind it.
3. Our record is public — what we got right and what we got wrong, in one place.
4. "Trust me" is never an answer. "Here, check for yourself" always is.
5. When we break trust, we say so first, in plain words, before anyone else finds out.

**Do:**

- Add proof to every claim: a link, a record, a number anyone can check again.
- Post our record — the wins and the mistakes — where anyone can read it, no permission needed.
- Write promises down, with dates, so anyone can compare what we said with what we did.
- Show how we got the answer, not just the answer.
- Say a promise is broken the same day we know it is broken.
- Put the correction in the same place, and just as big, as the original claim.
- Make checking easy: one page, plain words, no account needed.
- Put a date on everything, so old claims cannot pretend to be new.

**Don't:**

- Don't ask anyone to believe something they cannot check for themselves.
- Don't hide failures, bury them in small print, or quietly delete them.
- Don't use our name, titles, or size in place of proof.
- Don't make a promise if we cannot show we are keeping it.
- Don't delete the record of a mistake — fix it in the open and keep the history.
- Don't hide behind "that's confidential" (secret). If only one detail is truly secret — like a password — hide that detail and answer the rest.

**Why:**

Anyone can say "trust me" — liars say it most. Real trust works like a receipt: you don't have to believe the shopkeeper, because you can read what you paid and check it yourself. So we never ask for belief. We hand over the receipt: the claim, the proof, the dates, the mistakes next to the wins. And when we mess up, we tell you before you find out yourself. Trust that hides its cracks is not trust — it is a wall about to fall. A standard you can check is the only kind worth signing.

**The test:**

Can a stranger check our biggest claim today, alone, without asking us for anything?
